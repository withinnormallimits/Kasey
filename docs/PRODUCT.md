# Product rules

These are decisions, not preferences. Each one exists because of research or a bug we already hit. If a change would break one, stop and ask.

---

## 1. Capture is always retrospective

An episode is logged **after** the child is back to baseline, never during. This is a deliberate constraint and it buys us something: structured fields are affordable because nobody is filling them out under duress.

Consequence: backdating must be exact, obvious, and always correctable. A leading competitor silently files yesterday's entry under today and paywalls editing. That is the failure mode to avoid.

## 2. Recovery duration is the severity metric

Minutes back to baseline, not a 1 to 5 subjective rating. It is more objective, produces a clean median for the clinical summary, and is the number that makes "this strategy works" measurable.

## 3. Days and episodes are different things

- An **episode** is one moment. Several can happen in one day.
- A **good day** is the parent's verdict on the whole day.
- Both can be true on the same date. A rough half hour does not cancel a decent day.

**Count days as distinct calendar dates.** Counting rows produced a denominator larger than the number of days that existed, on the page handed to a psychiatrist. This bug shipped once. Do not let it back.

## 4. Escalation is a stage, not a list of behaviors

1. Caught it early — pulled out of it before it took hold
2. Shut down — went quiet, withdrew, stopped talking
3. Argued through it — defending, trying to regain control
4. Full meltdown — lost it completely

One tap instead of four. Makes episodes comparable across months. "He got to stage 2 and came back" is the win condition and it must be recordable.

## 5. Good days are logged, and there are no streaks

A record made only of meltdowns makes any child look worse than they are. Research: median 70% of users abandon lifestyle and mental health apps within 100 days. Lally et al. (2010) found that missing one occurrence does not materially affect habit formation, which is the case against punitive streaks.

One tap. Undo available. No guilt. Ever.

## 6. The generated page leads with who the child is

The sitter page opens with what they love, what they are proud of, what makes them laugh — **before** anything about triggers. Two reasons: the one-page-profile literature says lead with strengths, and a page that opens with "loves trains" reads as care while one that opens with "top triggers" reads as management.

## 7. No causal language

The section is "What comes before", never "What causes". At n≈40 a parent will conclude "red dye causes meltdowns" and the app will have manufactured that belief. Show frequency without implying cause. This is an ethical requirement, not a UX nicety.

Corollaries:
- Below 20 episodes, say so in plain language on the page.
- A strategy used once is labeled "(used once)" and ranked below ones with real evidence.
- Every before/after comparison carries the caveat that other things changed in the same window, including how consistently the parent logged.

## 8. Medications overlap

A child can be on Ritalin and melatonin at once. The earlier model ended each period when the next began, which silently recorded Ritalin as stopped the day melatonin started — a false clinical statement generated by a layout assumption.

Records carry: name, dose and schedule, start, optional end, and why. A dose change closes the old record and opens a new one so the sequence survives.

## 9. Local-first, and the user owns the data

- HIPAA does not apply to parent-entered consumer app data. Keep it that way.
- The FTC Health Breach Notification Rule **does** reach non-HIPAA health apps. Adding accounts or cloud sync brings breach-notification obligations.
- A behavioral record on a minor can surface in IEP disputes, school discipline, and custody proceedings. This objection from the autistic adult community is not abstract.

Export is explicit and user-initiated. Backup and restore is a **requirement**, not a nice-to-have: a lost phone must not destroy months of a child's medical history.

## 10. Language gets products torched

The autistic self-advocacy community reacts strongly to the puzzle piece, to "behavior / manage / tantrum / challenging behaviors", to framing the child as a problem to be fixed, and to collecting data without the autistic person's ownership.

One advocate's framing: the difference between filming a fire in 4K and handing someone a fire extinguisher. Logging distress without acting on it is useless or worse.

**Before public launch, two or three autistic adults must review the framing.** Treat their objections as blockers, not feedback.

## 11. Tone

Write like a parent reporting observations, not a medical system generating a report.

- Plain language. No clinical vocabulary in the interface.
- No em dashes or en dashes.
- No "it is worth noting", "notably", "this suggests", "consistent with".
- "Nothing we tried helped", never "nothing worked" — same data, does not read like a verdict on the parent at 11pm.
